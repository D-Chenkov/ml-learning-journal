**Learned:**
- Non-periodic signal: its frequency components CHANGE over time (no single fixed spectrum), unlike the periodic signals from ch.1-2. A chirp is the canonical example - a signal whose frequency varies (sweeps) over time.
- Frequency is the derivative of phase; phase is the integral of frequency. Constant frequency -> phase increases linearly (phi = 2*pi*f*t). For a chirp, f varies, so to GENERATE it you integrate f(t) to get phi(t) then take sin(phi). Instantaneous frequency = slope of the phase.
- STFT: chop the signal into short (windowed, often overlapping) segments and take the DFT of each, showing how the spectrum evolves in time.
- Spectrogram: visualizes the STFT. x = time, y = frequency, color = amplitude. A chirp shows up as a diagonal streak.
- Resolution: time resolution = n/r, frequency resolution = r/n (segment length n, frame rate r) - reciprocals. Longer segment = finer frequency but coarser time.
- Gabor limit: cannot be precise in time AND frequency at once. Short segment pinpoints WHEN but blurs WHAT frequency; long segment nails frequency but blurs when.
- Spectral leakage: the DFT assumes the segment is one period of a repeating signal; if the end does not connect smoothly to the start, that discontinuity injects spurious frequencies (energy leaks into neighboring bins).
- Windowing fixes leakage: multiply the segment element-wise by a window that tapers amplitude to ~zero at both edges (Hamming is common; Hann/Blackman are cousins). Trade-off: less leakage but a slightly wider main peak.
- Noise: white = equal power at all frequencies; pink = power ~ 1/f; Brownian/red = random walk (value = previous + step), power ~ 1/f^2. Gaussian is a DIFFERENT kind of descriptor - it describes the amplitude DISTRIBUTION (bell curve), not the spectrum. They are independent: "Gaussian white noise" has bell-curve values AND a flat spectrum. ("Brown" is from Brownian motion / Robert Brown, not the color.)
